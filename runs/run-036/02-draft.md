# Stop Compressing Your People: What AI Adoption Actually Demands of Your Talent Strategy

The instinct when AI shows up is to count what it can replace. The organizations that grow through this transition will count what it releases.

## The Cost-Reduction Framing Is the Wrong Starting Point

Most AI adoption conversations start with the same question: what can AI do that we currently pay people to do? That framing has a cost-reduction answer baked in, and it misses the most important variable.

**Compressible work and compressible people are different assets.** The product manager writing Jira tickets also holds the judgment about which ticket is worth writing. AI drafts fifty acceptance criteria in thirty seconds, then stalls on the question of which problem deserved thirty seconds in the first place. The person doing repeatable work carries institutional knowledge, customer pattern recognition, and contextual judgment that task automation leaves fully intact — and newly available.

**When this works:** Organizations that treat AI as a task offloader retain the institutional knowledge that expensive re-hiring cannot regenerate.  
**When it fails:** Organizations that reduce headcount based on task compression lose the domain knowledge and accumulated judgment that neither AI nor a new hire can replace quickly.

## What Prompting Actually Is — and Why Most People Do It Wrong

Blake McMillan, VP at Improving, runs AI workshops where participants arrive already disappointed. They tried AI, it underwhelmed them, and they quietly moved on. The training starts the same way every time: a vague, generic prompt, a bloated generic response, and a question to the room about how useful it was. "I could see where this could be useful," someone offers, half-heartedly.

Then he runs the identical request with full context — role, goal, constraints, format, the specific outcome needed. The consistent response: "Oh. *That's* why I was disappointed before."

**Prompting is delegation.** Asking AI for "something to drink" and expecting coffee with cream and sugar fails for the same reason asking a colleague the same way fails. Vague delegation produces any valid answer. Specific delegation produces the right one. Most people are poor delegators — AI makes that gap visible immediately and at scale.

## AI's Default Mode Is a Problem for Skill Development

While building AI courseware, Blake used AI to develop the curriculum — dog-fooding the tool he was teaching. Midway through, the model flagged a difficulty jump: "This might be a jump that many will find difficult." That critical observation appeared only because Blake had explicitly authorized the model to be critical. Without that instruction, the model would have validated the flawed curriculum enthusiastically and called it excellent.

**The golden retriever dynamic:** AI rewards prompts with affirmation by default. It validates bad ideas, weak drafts, and flawed logic with the same energy it brings to good work. Organizations deploying AI access without training people to override this default are generating a productivity illusion — confident output, unchallenged quality.

The skill that stays irreducibly human is evaluation. Ask for five options. Eliminate four. Redirect on the fifth. Verify before shipping. After AI enters the workflow, the human's primary job is critic — selecting, eliminating, redirecting, verifying.

## What This Demands Organizationally

AI has absorbed the meeting transcription role. Blake now embeds verbal keywords mid-meeting — "that was an action item," "I have a dependency on that" — to improve Copilot's summary quality. A human adapting their communication style to shape AI output in real time is the emerging competency class, and most organizations have no plan to develop it.

Released capacity requires a deliberate decision about what comes next. Organizations that invest in problem framing, customer understanding, synthesis, and cross-functional communication will convert that capacity into higher-value work. Organizations that simply compress headcount will find the same problems resurfacing with fewer people available to address them.

## The Case Against This Argument

Technology does eliminate job categories. Some displacement is real, and some roles will not have a higher-order opportunity waiting. The optimists undercount the structural support some people will need to reskill — support that goes beyond a training course and a license deployment.

The pessimists, though, locate people's value in their tasks. The institutional knowledge, the customer relationships, the accumulated pattern recognition — these live in the person, not the deliverable. Organizations that invest in people through transitions build adaptability and loyalty that AI cannot replicate and a new hire cannot rapidly regenerate.

AI creates capacity. What that capacity becomes is a deliberate organizational choice. The organizations making that choice now will be the ones with capable, loyal teams when the automation horizon actually matters.
