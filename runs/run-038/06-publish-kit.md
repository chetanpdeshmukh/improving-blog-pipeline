# Your AI ROI Framework Is the Problem

Six months into an AI program, Marcus Velez gets the same call. A CFO has reviewed the numbers and found nothing. Seven figures spent, and the monthly dashboard is unchanged. "Show me the ROI or we're pulling the funding." Marcus's first question back: what was the baseline before you started?

That question usually produces silence. When Marcus walks that team back through baseline definition, the conversation shifts from justification to diagnosis — and the program, more often than not, survives. The silence is the symptom. The missing baseline is the disease.

## Why the 1990s Software ROI Lens Breaks on AI

Most enterprises measure AI the same way they measured ERP implementations in 1998: cost savings, headcount reduction, process automation gains. That framework was designed for software with a fixed go-live date and immediate process changes. AI learns, compounds, and produces returns across a timeline that confounds quarterly budgets.

The operational efficiency gains most CFOs track are real. Strategic differentiation gains — decisions made with better information, faster market response — take six to eighteen months to appear in revenue. Organizational intelligence gains go unmeasured entirely because nobody built the instrumentation to see them. This includes institutional knowledge captured in systems and cross-functional pattern recognition that didn't exist before.

Organizations that define all three value dimensions before the program starts can track and communicate progress throughout. Organizations measuring only operational efficiency will pull funding at month six, right before compounding returns begin.

## Why Do AI Pilots Stall Before They Reach Production?

Across Improving's engagements, AI pilots consistently stall before production for the same three reasons: undocumented business processes the model can't handle, data governance gaps that make outputs unreliable, and a change management deficit that leaves end users without support or incentive to adopt.

A successful proof of concept with no scaling path is an expensive liability. The POC proved the technology works. Scaling requires proving the organization works around it.

**Failure mode:** Skipping organizational readiness assessment before declaring a pilot successful means you are measuring whether the model performs in controlled conditions. Production will not be controlled. Most programs that die in the pilot-to-production handoff were declared successful too early, against the wrong criteria.

## What Are AI Leaders Getting Wrong in CFO Conversations?

AI leaders are walking into CFO meetings without a time-horizon commitment, then losing credibility when month-six numbers don't move. The frame most executives carry into those conversations was built for ERP, where go-live produces visible process change in the next reporting cycle. AI returns arrive on a different schedule.

The three-phase arc looks like this:

**Phase 1 (Months 1-3):** Investment only. No return signal is visible. Track program setup, baseline documentation, and initial model training — not revenue.

**Phase 2 (Months 3-9):** The learning loop tightens. Adoption climbs, feedback improves model outputs, and baseline metrics begin to accumulate. Track adoption rate by user cohort, model accuracy trend, and time-to-insight improvement.

**Phase 3 (Month 9 onward):** Compounding begins. Financial results follow the leading indicator trends established in Phase 2. This is when the CFO conversation changes.

The board conversation has to name this arc before the program starts. A CFO expecting month-six revenue impact from a program that entered the learning phase at month four is measuring the wrong thing at the wrong time. Baseline definition and time-horizon alignment belong in the program charter, before spending begins.

## Leading Indicators Are What You Manage

Revenue impact and cost reduction are outcomes. Model accuracy trend, adoption rate by user cohort, and time-to-insight for key decisions are the indicators worth tracking from day one. These tell you whether the program is on track six months before the financial results confirm it.

**When this works:** Teams that report leading indicators to executive sponsors maintain credibility through the investment and learning phases. **When it fails:** Teams that promise financial outcomes and report nothing until those outcomes arrive create a trust gap that funding cuts tend to fill.

## The Change Management Ratio Nobody Gets Right

A useful rule of thumb from Improving's practice: one dollar in change management for every two dollars in technical build. Most organizations invert that ratio, allocating 80 percent of the budget to infrastructure and models and treating adoption as something that will happen on its own.

Change management in an AI program means process documentation before model integration, adoption architecture for end users, feedback loops from users to model teams, and sustained executive alignment. These are program functions that require explicit budget and ownership, not a line item added after the technical build is scoped.

The 1:2 ratio is calibrated for programs where end users drive the value realization. For a narrow automation initiative with minimal user interaction, the balance shifts. But if the program's returns depend on people changing how they work — and most do — the ratio holds.

**Failure mode:** Underfunding change management produces a technically functional system that nobody uses, which produces ROI matching the usage: zero.

## The Structural Advantage That Late Movers Cannot Buy

Organizations building governance, data foundations, and human-AI collaboration skills now will accumulate a structural advantage that compounds the same way their AI systems do. What late movers cannot purchase in 18 months is institutional model training history: the annotated data, production feedback loops, and tuning decisions that reflect a specific business environment. That corpus has to be grown.

Late movers also face a talent market that increasingly favors AI-native environments and a customer base whose expectations are already shaped by AI-native competitors. Neither gap closes by buying a larger model or a bigger vendor contract.

The measurement framework you build in the next twelve months determines which side of that gap you end up on. If you are currently running an AI program against a 1990s ROI lens, the most valuable thing you can fix this quarter has nothing to do with the technology.
