# The 70 Percent Problem: Why Most AI Programs Are Measuring the Wrong Things

A CFO walked into one of my conversations recently — two million dollars into an AI program, and the board wanted results. The numbers were not there. The working assumption was that the program had failed.

I asked two questions: What were you measuring before this started? And what timeline did the board agree to at launch?

Neither question had a clear answer. That silence was the problem, and it predated the AI investment by at least a year.

---

## You're Using the Wrong Scorecard

The framework most finance teams use to evaluate AI spend was built for a different kind of investment. Cost savings. Headcount reduction. Process cycle times. These are legitimate metrics — they capture real value. They also capture only the first of three distinct layers where AI generates returns.

**Operational efficiency** is the visible layer: tasks automated, costs reduced, decisions accelerated. It shows up in year-one reporting and represents roughly 20–30% of the total value available to organizations that deploy AI seriously.

**Strategic differentiation** is the second layer: speed-to-market advantages, innovation velocity, the capacity to make decisions that competitors cannot yet make. This takes longer to materialize and requires deliberate measurement to see.

**Organizational intelligence** is the third: AI-surfaced patterns about workforce dynamics, customer behavior, and operational risk that change how leadership makes decisions over time. Most organizations never build the instrumentation to capture this layer at all.

Organizations measuring only operational efficiency are leaving approximately 70–80% of the available value unmeasured. [*This estimate reflects assessment across AI engagements; the specific research base requires attribution before publication.*]

**When this works:** When the organization has defined all three value dimensions before the program launches and built leading indicators for each. The scorecard becomes a shared reference point for board conversations.

**When it fails:** When operational efficiency is the only reporting mechanism, the board sees empty cells in Q1 and Q2, concludes the program is underperforming, and pulls funding during the learning phase — exactly when the investment is working as designed.

## The Leading Indicator Problem: Tracking Outcomes You Can't See Yet

AI programs fail their board reviews because organizations measure lagging indicators — revenue impact, cost per decision — before those indicators have any reason to move. Six months into a properly-run AI deployment, lagging indicators are still flat. The program is in the learning phase.

The fix is a two-track scorecard. Lagging indicators for eventual board accountability. Leading indicators — model accuracy trends, adoption rates, time-to-insight improvements — for managing the program week to week.

Months one through three are an investment phase. Useful metrics are adoption velocity, baseline comparisons, and data quality signals. The program should show no financial returns yet, and reporting otherwise creates expectations the timeline cannot support.

Months three through nine are the learning phase. Process adherence, the rate at which teams are changing their work based on AI outputs, and feedback loop quality are what to watch. A program that looks flat on lagging indicators but shows high adoption and strong learning signals is on track.

Month nine and beyond is where compounding returns begin. Building the financial case for a board at month three sets up a month-six credibility problem.

**When this works:** When leading and lagging indicators are defined, baselined, and agreed upon by finance and the technology team before the program launches.

**When it fails:** When the scorecard is designed after the program starts. Baselines invented retrospectively do not hold up in board rooms, and the program enters a credibility deficit it rarely recovers from.

## Why Successful Pilots Stall at Scale

Nearly 60% of enterprise AI pilots never reach production. The technology in those programs typically worked. The proof of concept succeeded. Leadership got excited, approved expansion funding, and then the program stalled.

The stall happens for one of three reasons. Change management is inadequately funded. Data governance is immature for production-scale AI. Business processes the AI is supposed to integrate with are undocumented, leaving the system no stable surface to attach to.

The ratio I recommend — one dollar of change management for every two dollars of technical build — reflects observed failure patterns across AI programs. [*The specific evidence base behind this ratio requires follow-up before publication.*] Most organizations invert it, spending heavily on infrastructure and minimally on adoption support. The result is a functioning system that changes nothing about how the organization actually operates.

Data readiness is the single biggest gating factor I see before programs begin. Organizations that invest in AI infrastructure before addressing data quality end up with models that compound whatever inconsistency exists in the underlying data. A better model amplifies what is already there — clean inputs improve, bad inputs get confidently wrong.

**When this works:** When data readiness is assessed and addressed before infrastructure spend begins, and when the change management budget is treated as a program requirement alongside the technical build.

**When it fails:** When data readiness is treated as something to clean up after the model is deployed. At that point, the organization has committed to infrastructure that cannot perform, and the cleanup cost exceeds what it would have taken to address at the start.

## The Board Conversation You're Not Having

ERP implementations deliver visible process changes at go-live. AI systems improve over time — they get smarter as they process more data, and as the people using them get better at asking useful questions. These are structurally different investment types, and they require different board conversations.

When a CFO presents an AI investment to a board expecting year-one returns, the expectation is already wrong. Correcting it is a leadership problem with a leadership solution: establish the three-phase timeline at program launch, define what success looks like in each phase, and hold reporting to that structure.

Two questions worth asking before any AI program begins: What are we measuring today that we will measure again after deployment? And what timeline has the board agreed to for financial returns? If either answer is unclear, the program is launching on a faulty foundation.

**When this works:** When the board understands and has agreed to the investment/learning/compounding model before the first dollar is spent. Executive alignment on the timeline is itself a risk-reduction measure.

**When it fails:** When the board conversation happens after the program is running, in response to a demand for results. At that point, the conversation is a defense, and defenses are harder to win than agreements made at the start.

## The Talent Story Is Being Told Backwards

The dominant narrative about AI and the workforce leads with displacement — roles automated, headcount reduced. That story is real in some contexts. It is also the smaller of two stories, and leading with it does active damage to program adoption.

The more consequential pattern across AI deployments in knowledge worker environments is augmentation. Organizations that deploy AI tools correctly see 20–40% productivity improvement in those roles. Employee satisfaction trends upward. The work that remains is the work people were actually hired to do — analysis, judgment, creative problem-solving — cleared of the administrative and repetitive tasks that consumed their time before.

AI-surfaced workforce patterns also enable proactive talent development in ways that were not previously operationally feasible. This is organizational intelligence in practice: leadership making skills investment decisions based on signals the organization could not see before.

**When this works:** When AI deployment is designed around role augmentation and the change management investment includes clear communication about what the technology is for.

**When it fails:** When the displacement narrative is allowed to define employee perception of the program. Adoption collapses when the workforce believes the tool is measuring them for elimination, and collapsed adoption makes every other metric worse.

## The Skeptic's View — A Fair Challenge

A CFO with fiduciary responsibility has grounds to push back on these frameworks. The 70–80% unmeasured value estimate, the 60% pilot stall rate, the 1:2 change management ratio — these are aggregates from consulting engagements and may apply unevenly depending on the organization's industry, starting data position, and competitive context. The statistics are also made by people with an interest in more AI investment. That is a fair observation.

The honest response is that the frameworks are useful precisely because they surface questions the organization has to answer for itself. What were we measuring before? What time horizon did we agree on? Where does our data quality actually stand? What is the change management budget?

If those questions have clear answers before the program launches, the framework is doing its job. If they do not, that is the risk to address. The measurement problem precedes the technology problem, and fixing measurement after deployment is harder than fixing it before.

The window to build durable AI capability is narrowing. Early movers are compounding on their own data, their own model improvements, and their own organizational learning. None of that can be purchased quickly by a late mover.

The cost of getting measurement right upfront is low. The cost of the two-million-dollar conversation with no good answer is not.
