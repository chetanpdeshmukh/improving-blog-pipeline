# The AI Trap: Why Businesses That Chase Headlines Fall Behind

When a credit card chatbot waived my late fee without routing me to a human agent, my first reaction was surprise. Not because the AI worked, but because someone at that company had made a deliberate decision: this tool is authorized to resolve customer problems, not just acknowledge them. That distinction matters more than the technology behind it.

Most AI initiatives fail that test before they start. Organizations chase the announcement, buy the platform, and ask "how do we add AI?" The question frames AI as an additive layer rather than an amplifier of existing capability. The 30 to 40 percent productivity gains that researchers attribute to well-deployed AI do not appear for organizations that misread this framing. The 15 to 20 percent productivity losses do.

## Start With What Your Business Actually Does Well

The companies generating sustainable ROI from AI are finding friction points inside the model they already have and removing them. A roofing company partnered with a startup that used weather pattern analysis and satellite imagery to identify hailstorm-affected neighborhoods before competitors could mobilize. The roofing company's core business stayed exactly the same: show up, do good work, leave the roof better than you found it. AI made their targeting sharper. Every engagement they took on was already a strong match.

The investment math requires this clarity. If the tool amplifies something your business does well, the productivity gain compounds. If it displaces something customers valued in the first place, you have traded a differentiator for a cost line.

**When this works:** You have a clear, stable core capability and AI reduces the friction, latency, or cost of delivering it.

**When it fails:** The organization uses AI adoption to patch a broken process rather than fix it. The process remains broken, now with an AI wrapper and a budget line attached.

## The Content Your Organization Already Has But Cannot Use

One of the most consistent patterns Improving sees is enterprises sitting on large, unstructured content libraries that are functionally invisible. A company in the education sector had years of curriculum content: recorded videos, presentations, classroom aids, supporting materials. Clients needed to self-serve, locating content relevant to their specific context. The content existed; finding the right piece of it in a reasonable timeframe was practically impossible.

Improving used AI to index that content semantically, making it searchable across context and intent rather than keyword alone. The same library that already existed became accessible in ways its original structure had never supported. The specific ROI metrics for that engagement were not captured in the interview, which is an honest gap: the technology worked, and the business outcome numbers belong in a follow-up conversation with the team.

The principle holds regardless. AI applied to retrieval and synthesis of unstructured content produces value quickly because humans are genuinely bad at that work. Boredom and fatigue degrade the quality of a human search through 400 hours of video. AI does not experience either constraint.

The hallucination risk belongs in any honest assessment. A human reviewer at the end of the process is not optional. AI surfaces candidates; a practitioner makes the call.

**When this works:** Large content libraries, qualified retrieval criteria, and a human in the final decision loop.

**When it fails:** You deploy AI retrieval without access controls. The system will surface whatever it can reach, including compensation data, personnel files, or client contracts, to whoever asks the right question. This is a governance gap before it is a security gap.

## The Foundation Everyone Wants to Skip

A strong data estate is the prerequisite for every AI capability worth building. Improving's experience is consistent: organizations that attempt to build AI capabilities before answering basic data questions spend significant time and money discovering the gap rather than closing it.

The questions are organizational, not technical. What data do you have? Where does it live? Who can access which parts of it? What is sensitive and what is not? Which third-party platforms you have integrated also hold your data, and under what terms?

The inner ring fence problem is underappreciated. Organizations focus on perimeter security, keeping data from leaving the organization. An AI tool connected to internal data without role-based access controls defeats that perimeter from the inside. Executive compensation data accessible to the wrong query is not a hypothetical. It is a misconfigured permission set and a user who asked the right question.

**When this works:** Data catalog exists, classification is current, access controls reflect actual organizational roles, and third-party integrations are included in the governance scope.

**When it fails:** You connect AI to your data estate before completing the audit. At that point, AI accelerates discovery of your governance gaps by whoever is asking questions.

## The Line Between Consumer and Enterprise AI

An employee pasting a client report into their personal ChatGPT account is not making a security mistake in their own frame of reference. They are using a tool that works well and is free. The business risk belongs to the organization that left that line blurry.

Consumer AI tools operate under terms that permit using input data for model improvement. Enterprise agreements with Azure OpenAI, enterprise Anthropic, and enterprise Google accounts operate under different terms: training exclusion, processing boundaries, contractual accountability. These are not guarantees of perfect security. They are a material difference in data handling that organizations need to understand and communicate explicitly. Roughly 60 percent of companies have no AI-specific policy that draws this line. That gap is a business risk before it is a compliance risk.

**When this works:** The organization has a written policy distinguishing approved enterprise tools from consumer tools, with onboarding coverage and a named owner.

**When it fails:** The policy exists as a document but not as a communicated standard. Employees continue using consumer tools because nobody explained why the distinction mattered.

## Why Mandates Produce the Opposite of Adoption

Metric-driven AI mandates create resistance because employees read them as surveillance, not investment. "You must demonstrate X uses of AI per week" positions the tool as a performance monitoring mechanism. The resistance is rational, and it compounds: employees who feel monitored find the minimum threshold and stop there.

What actually drives adoption is showing practitioners how AI elevates the work they already care about doing well. Improving's approach is closer to a buffet: here are the tools, here are the boundaries, here is where other people have found value. The adoption that follows is genuine because people are solving their actual problems.

**When this works:** The organization leads with capability examples relevant to the specific roles being asked to adopt, before any metric is introduced.

**When it fails:** The mandate arrives before the example. Employees who do not yet understand what AI can do for them specifically read the mandate as pressure, not opportunity.

## Where You Actually Are

Most organizations sit somewhere in a three-level progression. Level 1 is conversational: AI as a capable research assistant, summarizer, and thinking partner. The quality of output is directly tied to the quality of context you provide. Most individuals and many organizations are here. Level 2 connects AI to internal data in a governed, access-controlled framework. The education company example lives here. Level 3 is agentic: AI that executes tasks with minimal instruction. This is where leverage is highest and where nobody has fully figured out the operational model yet.

The definitions above are rougher than a final publication warrants. A follow-up with John Rusic before the final draft would sharpen the distinctions between levels, particularly the boundary between Level 2 and Level 3. The core message is accurate: wherever your organization sits is a valid starting point.

The leaders who do this well ask one question before any other: what problem does this solve for our customers? That question determines whether AI earns its paycheck or joins the list of expensive initiatives that made great slides at the all-hands.
