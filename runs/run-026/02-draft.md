# AI Works Best When It Amplifies What You Already Do Well

A few months ago, John Rusic missed a credit card payment. He opened the company's chatbot expecting the usual runaround, explained the situation, and the bot refunded the late fee immediately with no escalation required. Most chatbots are complaint filters, not resolution tools. This one had actual authorization to solve the problem, and that distinction is a business decision first, a technology decision second.

The businesses getting AI right make exactly that call before they touch a tool. They identify what they do well, find where AI can do that work faster or at greater scale, and stop there. The businesses getting it wrong start with the technology and work backward to a use case. That path produces chatbots that answer nothing useful, dashboards that surface nothing actionable, and productivity investments that return nothing measurable.

## Where the ROI Actually Lives

The strongest demonstrated return from AI right now is in unstructured data retrieval: surfacing knowledge from content libraries that technically exist but are practically invisible. AI does not get bored doing this work. Humans do, which is why the work either does not get done or gets done badly.

One education company Improving worked with had a massive library of video content, presentations, and classroom materials. Clients could not find what they needed. The content existed; the discoverability did not. After Improving indexed and semantically searched that library using AI, clients could locate relevant material in ways no keyword search could replicate. The business impact was closing the gap between "we have this" and "anyone can actually use this."

A roofing startup makes the same point from a different angle. Roofers need to identify high-opportunity markets quickly after weather events. The startup used AI to analyze weather patterns, map hailstorm-affected areas, and help roofers reach those markets before competitors arrived. The underlying business model, fast localized sales after weather damage, was already there. AI made the targeting faster and more precise.

**When this works:** AI retrieval and synthesis delivers measurable value when an organization has a large, established body of knowledge that people need but cannot efficiently navigate.

**When it fails:** Retrieval quality depends entirely on the quality of the underlying data. Connecting AI to a disorganized, untagged data estate produces confident, wrong answers rather than useful ones.

## The Foundation Most Companies Skip

A strong data estate is the prerequisite for every AI capability worth building. Most organizations discover this gap after they have already tried to build something on top of it.

The starting questions are practical: what data do you have, where does it live, who can access it, and which parts are sensitive? Business leaders own these answers. Companies that cannot answer them are not ready to build AI capabilities. They are ready to catalog.

The more urgent version of this problem is internal access control. An AI tool without ring fences around sensitive data will surface anything it can reach to whoever asks the right question. Connect an ungoverned AI to an HR platform and a marketing analyst can ask about executive compensation and get an accurate answer. That exposure exists the moment you connect AI to the data, not after someone asks.

**When this works:** Organizations that have invested in cataloging, tagging, and governing their data estate can move quickly once AI tools are connected. Each investment in foundation pays dividends across every capability built on top of it.

**When it fails:** Rushing to connect AI to ungoverned data creates a security liability that scales with the AI's capabilities. No amount of perimeter security compensates for missing internal access controls.

## The Consumer AI Line Leaders Need to Draw

Enterprise-licensed AI tools and consumer AI tools operate under fundamentally different contractual terms. Consumer tools at the free or personal tier use input data for model improvement. That is the business model. An employee pasting a client report into their personal account has donated that data, regardless of intent. The liability is proportional to how sensitive the material was.

Enterprise agreements with Azure OpenAI, enterprise Anthropic accounts, and enterprise Google accounts include training exclusion clauses, defined processing boundaries, and contractual accountability. These are material differences from consumer terms, and the legal exposure for companies that have not drawn this line is not theoretical.

Roughly 40 percent of companies have a formal AI policy. The other 60 percent have employees making their own calls about which tools to use and what to put into them.

**When this works:** A clear internal policy, tied to enterprise agreements employees can actually access, reduces exposure and gives people a compliant path to the productivity gains they are already seeking on their own.

**When it fails:** Policy without accessible alternatives drives employees toward consumer tools anyway. A prohibition that offers no replacement produces evasion, not compliance.

## Why Fast Development Still Needs Old Discipline

AI coding agents optimize for working code. Security posture is a separate variable they do not prioritize. The decades of practice around code reviews, credential management, and security scans did not become optional because development accelerated. They became more important because the code surface area expands faster.

The specific failure mode: an AI agent building out a feature will hardcode credentials in accessible locations if that is the path of least friction. The code works. It also creates a vulnerability that security review exists to catch. Compress the review because the code arrived quickly, and you have moved fast toward a breach.

**When this works:** Teams that treat AI as an accelerator for the development phase and maintain existing security checkpoints get faster delivery without trading security for speed.

**When it fails:** Teams that interpret AI-assisted development as permission to compress the security process discover the liability only after something breaks in production.

## What Mandate-Driven Adoption Gets Wrong

Metric-driven AI mandates generate resistance rather than adoption. Employees who receive "you must use AI, and we are measuring it" directives read that as performance monitoring. The framing makes AI something happening to them rather than something available for them.

Adoption that produces changed workflows looks different. It starts by showing people what AI enables for work they already care about doing well. Give people the right tools, explain where the data boundaries are, and let them find where AI helps specifically. The mandate approach produces usage metrics. The capability approach produces durable changes in how work gets done.

**When this works:** Capability-first adoption works when leadership can demonstrate concrete examples in the team's specific context, not generic productivity statistics pulled from industry reports.

**When it fails:** Without visible examples and accessible tools, even well-framed capability messaging reads as hype. Concrete demonstrations matter more than the framing around them.

## Where Most Organizations Actually Are

Most organizations are at Level 1: individuals using AI as a research and synthesis tool, with output quality determined by the context they provide. Level 2 is AI connected to internal data in a governed, access-controlled framework. Level 3 is agentic AI: systems that execute tasks autonomously, with or without human instruction before each action.

Nobody has fully solved Level 3. The goal is understanding which level you are at and taking the next step deliberately, not announcing a Level 3 initiative because a competitor did.

## The Argument Against This

The strongest counter-argument is that "amplify your DNA" can rationalize moving too slowly. Early AI movers are building capabilities that compound. The cost of waiting is not linear. And some business DNA is bad DNA. Protecting customer service processes that frustrate customers because they have always worked that way is stagnation, not preservation.

That counter-argument is correct about the risk. Where it misses is the alternative it implies. The thesis here is a corrective to AI theater: chatbots nobody needed, dashboards that surface nothing, initiatives whose primary output is a press release. The alternative to AI theater is intentional adoption, starting with the question every AI decision should answer first: what problem does this solve for our customers?

Leaders who can answer that before they answer the build-or-buy question are building something durable. Leaders who cannot are building something expensive.
