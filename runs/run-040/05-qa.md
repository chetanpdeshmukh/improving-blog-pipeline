### FAIL Items (Fix Before Publishing)

**1. Word count under minimum (Checkpoint 4A)**
The article is approximately 745 words, which is below the 800-word floor. The article is dense and substantive, so the fix is depth, not padding. The two most obvious expansion points are the "world turned into the red" story (see WARN item 2) and the networking section, which describes the problem but doesn't walk through what a real architecture upgrade looks like.

**2. No publication kit (Checkpoint 5D)**
The article is missing the SEO title tag (≤60 characters), meta description (≤155 characters), URL slug, and at least one internal Improving.com link. The checklist requires all four. These must be added as a separate section at the end of the document before the article can move to publication.

**3. "table stakes" — banned phrase (Checkpoint 3B)**
Location: "A fallback hierarchy of primary model, lower-capability fallback, and cached response is table stakes for any deployment where load can spike."
Fix direction: Replace with a concrete consequence. Example: "...is a baseline requirement; skip it and a traffic spike cascades to total service unavailability."

**4. Skeptic's Case restates body content verbatim (Checkpoint 4D)**
Location 1: Body — "Data center network requirements at inference scale are a category jump from what most distributed systems teams have encountered." Skeptic's Case — "Network bandwidth at inference scale is a category jump from standard data center design." Same phrasing, same point, same section of the article.
Location 2: "The patterns that survive production" establishes circuit breakers and fallback routing. The Skeptic's Case opens by restating that these patterns transfer, adding no new information.
Fix direction: The Skeptic's Case should lead with the counterargument that an experienced distributed systems team would actually make, then concede. Right now it opens by agreeing with the body before pivoting. Cut the restatement; start with the objection.

### WARN Items (Recommended Fixes)

**1. Target audience not named (Checkpoint 1B)**
The article is clearly for senior engineers and architects with distributed systems backgrounds, but a reader encountering it cold doesn't see that stated. A single line in the intro or a subhead would anchor it.

**2. Improving's experience is invisible (Checkpoint 1D)**
Amon is never identified as being at or associated with Improving. Craig is identified as Cloudflare. A reader has no way to know this represents Improving's perspective. One sentence of context ("Amon, a principal architect at Improving...") resolves this without making the article feel promotional.

**3. Timeline claim is unattributed (Checkpoint 2C)**
Location: "Teams that catch these gaps early spend a week redesigning. Teams that catch them six months in spend a quarter rebuilding."
This is a specific operational claim presented as fact. If this came from Amon's experience, attribute it. If it's editorial interpolation, either remove it or reframe as an observed pattern.

**4. Four requirements section follows mirror structure (Checkpoint 3C)**
Each of the four requirements sub-sections follows: bold header → 2–3 sentences of explanation → SME quote. This is predictable enough to read as templated. Breaking the structure once — leading with the quote, or cutting the bold header on one section — disrupts the pattern without losing the content.

**5. "World turned into the red" needs narrative expansion (Checkpoint 4C)**
Location: "Amon watched this happen in real time during a period when, as he describes it, 'the world turned into the red' following a significant market shift in available model capabilities and pricing assumptions."
This is the most vivid moment in the article and it gets one sentence. What was the infrastructure decision that got invalidated? What did they have to redo? What was the cost? Even two more sentences of narrative tension would make this land.

**6. "Category jump" is vague (Checkpoint 4E)**
Used in the body and the Skeptic's Case. Neither usage tells the reader what the jump actually is — from what baseline, to what requirement, under what conditions. The 10Gbps / 200Gbps numbers in the networking section are the right model; apply the same specificity to the phrase itself or replace it.

**7. No question-format H2s (Checkpoint 5A)**
None of the four H2s match how a search query is phrased. Adding one or two question-format H2s (e.g., "Why doesn't horizontal scaling work for GPU clusters the same way it works for microservices?") would improve AI citation capture without changing the article's substance.

**8. No snippet-ready answer structures (Checkpoint 5B)**
Follows from 5A. Once a question H2 is in place, the first 2–3 sentences under it should answer the question directly before expanding.

**9. No tables, numbered steps, or decision frameworks (Checkpoint 5E)**
The four requirements would work as a table with columns for the pattern, why it fails, and the replacement approach. This is also the single highest-impact change for AI citation readiness.

**10. Two technical accuracy items for SME verification (Checkpoint 6A)**
- "Anthropic routes Claude traffic to alternative models when primary capacity is overloaded" is cited as "visible evidence" but is actually an inference about Anthropic's internal routing. This needs either a public source or a reframe as observed behavior rather than stated fact.
- "Traditional distributed systems run on 10Gbps" understates current baselines. Many modern enterprise data centers already run 25–100Gbps. Qualifying this ("legacy distributed systems designs optimized for 10Gbps...") avoids the technical imprecision.
