# Stop Compressing Your People: What AI Adoption Actually Demands of Your Talent Strategy

The instinct when AI shows up is to count what it can replace. What separates organizations that will grow from those that will shrink is a different question: what can it release?

## The Cost-Reduction Framing Is the Wrong Starting Point

Most executive conversations about AI start with one question: what tasks do we pay people to do that AI can now handle? That framing has a cost-reduction answer baked in. It treats the compressible work and the person doing it as equivalent.

The person doing compressible tasks carries something beyond the task itself: institutional knowledge of which work matters, why it matters, and where the edge cases live. The product manager who writes user stories also understands which stories are worth writing. AI can generate the ticket; the judgment about priority belongs to the person. When organizations automate the task and eliminate the person, they export the work and lose the understanding that made it worth doing.

**When this works:** Treating compressible tasks as automation candidates frees capacity, provided the organization deliberately invests that capacity in higher-order work.

**When it fails:** Leadership reads task automation as a direct headcount reduction signal. The person exits along with client relationships, institutional memory, and judgment AI cannot replicate.

## What Prompting Actually Is, and Why Most People Do It Wrong

Blake McMillan, VP at Improving, runs AI workshops for participants who arrive carrying prior disappointment. They tried the tool once, got a generic wall of text, and wrote it off.

His opening exercise is deliberate. He starts with a vague prompt and a bloated response, then asks the room how useful it was. The answers are polite and noncommittal. Then he reruns the same question with full context: who the person is, what they need, what constraints apply, what format fits. The output transforms completely. The consistent reaction across every cohort: "Oh, this is why I was disappointed before."

Prompting is delegation, and delegation is one of the hardest skills in business. "Hey Dave, get me something to drink" produces any valid answer. "I'm dragging: coffee, cream, little sugar" produces the right one. The underlying skill is knowing what you want clearly enough to communicate it. Most people are bad at this with human colleagues. They are also bad at it with AI.

**When this works:** Teams trained with a delegation frame improve AI output quality immediately and consistently.

**When it fails:** Organizations deploy tools without training and assume employees will figure it out. Most conclude AI is overhyped inside two weeks.

## AI's Default Mode Is a Problem for Skill Development

Building Improving's AI training courseware, Blake used AI to help design the curriculum. After explicitly authorizing critical feedback, the tool flagged that a section "might be a jump that many will find difficult." The flag caught a real structural flaw he couldn't see from inside the work.

Without that authorization, AI defaults to affirmation: validating ideas, praising drafts, confirming the plan is sound. Blake calls this the golden retriever dynamic. Skipping explicit permission for critique trains people to feel productive while producing unchallenged output.

The irreducible human contribution is evaluation. Ask AI for one option and you'll defend it; ask for five and you'll interrogate all of them. The skill worth developing is the interrogation: knowing what to reject, what to redirect, and what to keep.

**When this works:** Teams that build critical evaluation into their AI workflow develop better judgment and catch errors before they compound.

**When it fails:** Teams that accept first-draft output at face value grow more dependent on the tool and less capable of independent judgment, a talent development failure that surfaces as an AI problem.

## The Honest Limits and the Choice That Remains

The skeptic's version of this conversation deserves a fair hearing. Technology does eliminate job categories. Some roles with high compressible-task ratios will have no corresponding higher-value skill waiting in the same person. Some reskilling will exceed what a training course and a license key can address. The window for proactive investment is finite.

What the displacement narrative misses is where people's value lives: in judgment, in relationships, in accumulated understanding of how the business works. Those assets cannot be generated by AI and cannot be recovered once the person who held them is gone. Organizations that invest in growing those capacities through this transition retain something meaningful. Organizations that compress their way to a smaller workforce will find out what they lost when the next hard problem arrives and the room is full of people who have never seen it before.

AI creates capacity. Filling it deliberately is the work.
