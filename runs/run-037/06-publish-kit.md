# Stop Compressing Your People: What AI Adoption Actually Demands of Your Talent Strategy

For executives deciding how AI changes what their workforce should look like, the first question usually forecloses the right answer. The instinct when AI shows up is to count what it can replace. Organizations that stop at that question find a cost reduction. The more productive question is what AI releases.

---

## Why the Cost-Reduction Frame for AI Gets Organizations Into Trouble

Most executive conversations about AI start with one question: what tasks do we pay people to do that AI can now handle? That framing has a cost-reduction answer baked in. It treats the compressible work and the person doing it as equivalent.

The person doing compressible tasks carries something beyond the task itself: institutional knowledge of which work matters, why it matters, and where the edge cases live. The product manager who writes user stories also understands which stories are worth writing. AI can generate the ticket; the judgment about priority belongs to the person. When organizations automate the task and eliminate the person, they export the work and lose the understanding that made it worth doing.

Done well, treating compressible tasks as automation candidates frees capacity that the organization deliberately reinvests in higher-order work—strategy, client relationships, and the problem-solving that requires knowing how the business actually functions. Done poorly, leadership reads task automation as a headcount reduction signal, and the person exits along with client relationships, institutional memory, and judgment AI cannot replicate.

## What Does Prompting Actually Require—And Why Do Most People Get It Wrong?

Prompting is a delegation skill. Most people are as bad at it with AI as they are with colleagues—and that gap explains why most first attempts with AI tools fail.

Blake McMillan, VP at Improving, runs AI workshops for participants who arrive carrying prior disappointment. They tried the tool once, got a wall of generic text that could apply to any situation in any company, and wrote it off.

His opening exercise is deliberate. He starts with a vague prompt and watches the tool produce three paragraphs of advice so broad it offers no real leverage—the kind of output that makes a thoughtful person conclude the technology is overhyped. He asks the room how useful it was. The answers are polite and noncommittal. Then he reruns the same question with full context: who the person is, what they need, what constraints apply, what format fits. The output transforms completely. The consistent reaction across every cohort: "Oh, this is why I was disappointed before."

The underlying skill is knowing what you want clearly enough to communicate it. "Hey Dave, get me something to drink" produces any valid answer. "I'm dragging: coffee, cream, little sugar" produces the right one. Most people are bad at this with human colleagues. They are also bad at it with AI.

**How to prompt AI the way you delegate to a senior colleague:**

1. Establish context first. Tell the tool who you are, what your actual goal is, and what you already know. Skip this and you get generic.
2. Specify constraints and format. What can't change? What should the output look like? A busy executive can't turn a wall of text into a usable decision.
3. Authorize criticism explicitly. Default AI behavior is affirmation. If you want the tool to challenge your thinking, say so directly—otherwise it will agree with whatever you propose.

Across Improving's AI workshops, product and delivery teams that learn the delegation frame within a single session consistently produce usable first drafts where they previously got walls of generic text. Organizations that deploy tools without training and assume employees will figure it out hit the same wall: people try the tool once, get a generic response, and conclude AI is overhyped inside two weeks.

## Why Does AI Default to Affirmation—And What Does That Cost Your Team?

AI's default behavior is validation. Without an explicit instruction to push back, the tool will confirm your ideas, praise your drafts, and agree that your plan is sound—regardless of whether any of that is true.

Building Improving's AI training courseware, Blake used AI to help design the curriculum. After explicitly authorizing critical feedback, the tool flagged that a section "might be a jump that many will find difficult." The flag caught a real structural flaw he couldn't see from inside the work. Without the intervention, that section would have shipped as written, and participants in the next cohort would have hit a comprehension wall that Blake would have attributed to the material's difficulty rather than its sequencing—a root cause that would have taken two or three cohorts to identify.

Blake calls the default behavior the golden retriever dynamic. Skipping explicit permission for critique trains people to feel productive while producing unchallenged output.

The irreducible human contribution is evaluation. Ask AI for one option and you'll defend it; ask for five and you'll interrogate all of them. The skill worth developing is the interrogation: knowing what to reject, what to redirect, and what to keep.

Teams that build critical evaluation into their AI workflow catch structural errors before they reach clients—the kind that, left uncaught, create rework, erode trust, and get attributed to the technology rather than the process. Teams that accept first-draft output at face value grow more dependent on the tool and less capable of independent judgment. That's a talent development failure that surfaces as an AI problem.

## The Honest Limits and the Choice That Remains

The skeptic's objection here is legitimate. Technology does eliminate job categories. Some roles with high compressible-task ratios will have no corresponding higher-value skill waiting in the same person. Some reskilling will exceed what a training course and a license key can address. The window for proactive investment is finite.

The displacement narrative misses where people's value actually lives: in judgment, in relationships, in accumulated understanding of how the business works. Those assets cannot be generated by AI and cannot be recovered once the person who held them is gone. Organizations that invest in growing those capacities keep them intact. Organizations that compress their way to a smaller workforce discover the gap when the next hard problem arrives and the room is full of people who have never seen it before.

AI creates capacity. Filling it deliberately is the work.
