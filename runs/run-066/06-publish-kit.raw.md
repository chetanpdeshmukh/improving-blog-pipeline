# Nearshore Pricing Hasn't Caught Up With AI Velocity

David Sandoval has run Improving Mexico's engineering practice for fifteen years, and he is tired of one conversation: the cost-per-hour negotiation. "If you're only looking at how much is it going to cost me per hour," Sandoval says, "that's probably not going to be the best play for a pure nearshore team." The argument used to be theoretical. AI made it a live experiment, and the results arrive unevenly depending on how organizations treated adoption: as a licensing event or an operating model change.

---

## Why Did the Nearshore Labor Arbitrage Model Stop Working?

AI decoupled engineer headcount from engineer output. A single engineer with the right workflow can now produce at two to three times prior velocity, which makes cost-per-hour a lagging indicator of almost nothing useful. The metric that matters now is cost-per-outcome: what did this team ship, in what timeframe, with what quality signal?

The labor arbitrage pitch worked for twenty years: hire at a fraction of San Francisco rates and pocket the difference. Mexico produces approximately 130,000 STEM graduates annually, in Sandoval's estimate based on fifteen years of hiring into that pipeline, a talent pool most U.S. enterprises evaluated solely on time zone and price. Capability rarely entered the negotiation.

Organizations that already measure delivery through sprint metrics or DORA baselines (engineering velocity metrics covering deployment frequency, lead time, and failure recovery time) find this transition natural. AI changes the numerator; the measurement already exists. Without that baseline, "cost-per-outcome" remains abstract precisely when executive confidence in the engagement is most at risk. That baseline work is not optional preparation for AI adoption. It is the prerequisite for knowing whether AI adoption worked.

---

## AI Amplifies What Your Team Already Is

Sandoval frames the dynamic precisely: "AI is going to sort of serve as a mirror to whatever already is happening in the culture of the team and their activities." A team with clear process, good review habits, and collaborative trust will see AI compound their output. A team running on ambiguous requirements and low accountability will see AI accelerate those failure modes.

The sequencing mistake most organizations make is treating AI as the intervention. The AI engineering framework, which covers operating model design, role clarity, output review practices, and prompt governance, has to exist before the tools are introduced. Distribute licenses into a team without that foundation and the budget disappears quickly, with no clear accounting for what it produced. Sandoval calls one version of this the "Candy Crush competitor" pattern: AI spend at scale, zero business alignment, no accountability for outcomes.

When an engineering culture and documented workflow practices already exist, layering AI tooling on top produces measurable velocity gains within a sprint cycle. Without prompt governance or output review protocols, unreviewed AI-generated code enters production and the failure surface grows with adoption speed, silently, before it shows up on any dashboard.

## What Actually Happened When One Nearshore Team Went All-In on AI

A pharma logistics company in Dallas engaged Improving Mexico across multiple teams. One team was selected for full AI integration across task understanding, releases, monitoring, quality testing, and verification, while the others continued as-is. The client's intent was a controlled comparison: measure the AI-enabled team against the rest of the engagement as baseline.

The pressure was an executive mandate to quantify results. The selected team brought what Sandoval describes as "the will to try and experiment and challenge assumptions," a prerequisite the team itself had to supply. The AI engineering framework was what Improving Mexico provided.

The team built a CLI AI agent integrated directly into their support ticketing system. The agent read incoming tickets, surfaced contextual history, generated action items, and auto-drafted emails to account contacts. Ticket resolution time dropped from 40 minutes to 4 minutes. Some team members saw 50 to 60 percent productivity gains across their broader workload. (Editorial note: confirm the specific measurement basis with Sandoval before publishing, for example story points per sprint, DORA velocity, or another tracked metric, and add that framing here.)

Results were uneven across individuals, which Sandoval presents as the honest story of AI adoption in a real team. That unevenness is signal, not failure. It surfaces what is working and what is not in near-real time. A pilot treated as a performance evaluation produces a different outcome: engineers optimize their individual metrics, the dashboard looks strong, and the operating model stays unchanged underneath it.

## The Risks Nobody Puts in the Brochure

Uneven adoption within a single team is the most underrated risk Sandoval names. One engineer goes deep on AI; teammates continue their prior workflow and skip reviewing AI-generated outputs. The security exposure from unreviewed code entering production scales with how aggressively the outlier is producing. Sandoval's teams have seen this pattern introduce vulnerabilities that a standard peer review would have caught.

The distributed hiring problem compounded this. During a video interview, a candidate appeared to answer questions live. The interviewing team noticed the lip sync: someone off-screen was providing the answers in real time. AI lowered the barrier for this kind of deception. Improving Mexico's response was to resequence hiring: cultural alignment and growth mindset come first, foundational engineering competency second, AI proficiency third. Engineers who can perform without AI available are engineers who can evaluate what AI produces.

Tools like Ropes AI, which monitors screen capture and background processes during assessments (editorial note: confirm this product description with Sandoval before publishing), add a detection layer. Human judgment and engineering fundamentals remain the primary screen. A hiring process built on detection tooling alone will eventually surface teams built on undiscovered gaps.

The downstream result is a two-tier team: high-speed producers alongside reviewers unprepared to evaluate what they are approving.

## The Pendulum: Where Should Your Organization Actually Sit?

Sandoval uses a pendulum to frame the executive decision. The left anchor is full headcount with no AI, leaving velocity and capability gains unrealized. The right anchor is full automation with minimal team, trading cultural continuity and alignment for throughput. Both positions carry real costs, and the choice requires deliberate calibration.

The middle position is an AI-enabled operating model built on nearshore capability. A well-run nearshore team with a strong AI operating model captures both the productivity gains and the cultural, timezone, and collaboration advantages of nearshore delivery simultaneously.

The question that determines where to position: where in your workflow does judgment need to happen, and where is explicit task definition sufficient? The first category belongs to the human layer. The second is the compression target for AI tooling. Organizations that define this thinking-to-execution ratio, the proportion of work requiring judgment versus work that can be explicitly specified and delegated, can automate task compression while preserving judgment-intensive collaboration in the human layer. Organizations that skip this step hold a pendulum with no framework underneath it, and the swing becomes unpredictable.

## How Do You Evaluate an AI-Ready Nearshore Partner?

Ask one question before engaging: show me your AI engineering framework. A credible answer names specific artifacts: prompt governance documentation, token spend observability, and the output review process before code ships.

The key distinction is whether a partner has already run internal experiments or is building their AI operating model alongside your engagement. A partner with completed pilots can describe a specific experiment, name the metric they tracked, and tell you what changed as a result. Use this framework to categorize any response you receive:

| Partner Stage | What They Can Demonstrate | What It Means for You |
|---|---|---|
| Ahead of the curve | Internal pilot results, specific metrics, prompt governance docs | You inherit their learning, not their risk |
| Building alongside you | Program in progress, no completed pilots | You share the learning curve cost |
| Slide deck only | AI strategy in presentation form, no operational artifacts | You are the experiment |

The upskilling question matters equally. AI capability in engineering teams requires a structured program. A nearshore partner who built that program before you engaged them brings the learning curve advantage to the engagement. A partner building it alongside you is charging you for their own education. A partner whose AI strategy exists only in slide decks means inheriting their experiment as your production risk.

---

## Will AI Eventually Make Nearshore Irrelevant?

No. AI does not make nearshore irrelevant. It moves collaboration higher in the value chain, concentrating the remaining work where it requires judgment, alignment, and cultural trust, which is exactly what a well-run nearshore team provides.

The credible objection is real: if AI can compress tasks, summarize meetings, translate documents, and generate code at scale, why does a distributed team add value? Sandoval's answer is that task compression does not eliminate the need for human judgment at delivery scale; it concentrates judgment-intensive work in the human layer. As AI absorbs explicit, well-scoped work, the value of a team that can hold context, navigate ambiguity, and align across time zones increases rather than decreases.

The cost of reducing that human layer shows up slowly. Sandoval describes a pattern he has seen repeatedly: organizations that downsize nearshore headcount in favor of AI throughput discover the alignment gap during a critical delivery window, when there is no time to rebuild what was dismantled. The speed lost to restoring shared context and trust at that moment exceeds whatever throughput was gained by reducing the team. The accumulated cost becomes visible only when a deadline makes it unavoidable.

Sandoval acknowledges that AI gains are currently uneven across individuals, teams, and engagements. Organizations that act on that acknowledgment now are revising their partner selection criteria and building AI adoption standards into contract expectations. The gap between those organizations and the ones deferring is widening.

AI has already reshaped nearshore delivery. The question executives face now is whether their current nearshore partner is the kind of team that reshaping revealed as worth keeping.
