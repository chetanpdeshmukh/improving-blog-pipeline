# AI Doesn't Change Your Business DNA. It Amplifies It.

The roofing company in John Rusic's story has no data scientists. No machine learning engineers. No AI strategy deck with a three-year roadmap. What they have is a startup that figured out roofers need to knock on doors within 48 hours of a hailstorm, and that AI can tell them exactly which neighborhoods to target before the competition shows up.

"I've spent the last several years watching businesses try to add AI to things that were working fine and abandon initiatives that could have compounded what they were already doing well," says Rusic, Technical Director at Improving's AI Practice. The organizations that get results ask a different first question: what do we already do well, and where does AI help us do it faster or at a scale we couldn't reach before? The organizations that don't get results ask what AI they should deploy.

That framing difference decides whether you land in the 30-40% productivity gain cohort or the 15-20% productivity loss cohort. The math does not favor ambiguity.

## Where AI Earns Its Paycheck

Improving worked with an education company sitting on a massive library of content: videos, PowerPoint presentations, classroom aids built over years of curriculum development. Clients who paid for access to that content couldn't effectively find what was relevant to them. The library existed. The knowledge was functionally invisible.

The pressure was straightforward: clients needed to self-serve and locate material specific to their context at the moment they needed it. Manually curating content for each client wasn't economically viable. Improving used AI to index and semantically search across that unstructured content, making it discoverable in ways traditional keyword search couldn't touch. A client looking for curriculum material on a specific topic could surface relevant resources from a library that would have taken hours to manually browse.

The roofing example makes the principle more visible. A startup partnered with HVAC and roofing companies to analyze weather pattern data, identify areas hit by hailstorms, and help roofers target marketing before competitors arrived. Roofing has no obvious AI use case until you realize the actual business problem is timing. The company's DNA was speed of market response. AI amplified it.

**When this works:** Your business has accumulated content, data, or institutional knowledge that is genuinely valuable but inaccessible at scale. AI handles the synthesis and retrieval. The decision of what to do with that information stays with your people. Critically, a human reviewer sits at the end of that process for anything customer-facing. AI still fabricates. Skip the review step and you have traded accuracy for speed, which is rarely the bargain you intended.

**When it fails:** The content or data you're surfacing is ungoverned. AI returns whatever it can reach, to whoever asks the right question. Which leads directly to the next problem.

## The Foundation Nobody Wants to Build First

Every AI capability worth building sits on top of a data estate most companies have not built yet. That is a business strategy problem wearing IT clothes.

The starting question is blunt: do you know what data you have, where it lives, who can access it, and what is sensitive? Most organizations cannot answer all four parts with confidence. They discover the gaps when they connect AI to their data and something surfaces that should not have.

The specific failure mode: an AI tool without internal access controls returns anything it can reach to whoever asks the right question. A marketing team member queries the internal knowledge base and the response includes executive compensation data. The outer security perimeter held. The inner ring fences did not exist. This is a data governance gap that became visible the moment you gave employees a tool smart enough to find the information.

Third-party data compounds the risk. If your HR platform or SaaS tools are integrated into your data estate, their data governance terms are now your liability too.

**When this works:** You have cataloged, tagged, and classified your data before connecting AI to it. Access controls are role-based and tested. You know what is sensitive and who can reach it.

**When it fails:** You build AI capabilities on top of an ungoverned data estate and discover the access control gaps through an incident rather than an audit.

## The Consumer AI Line Your Organization Must Draw

There is a material difference between an employee using a personal ChatGPT account and an employee using an enterprise Azure OpenAI deployment. The business model of consumer AI tools is not productivity; it is data. An employee who pastes a client report or internal financial summary into a consumer AI tool has potentially donated that data to a model training process. One industry analysis cited 232 data leakage incidents per day on average across organizations. Rusic's response: "That seems low to me." Source that stat before publishing it, but the directional point is confirmed by what enterprise clients report.

Enterprise agreements purchase training data exclusion, processing boundaries, and contractual accountability. They don't guarantee perfect protection, but they represent a materially different risk profile than consumer terms.

The 60% of organizations with no AI or GPT-specific policy are not making a neutral choice. They are letting employees navigate this distinction individually, without guidance, at productivity speeds.

**When this works:** Leadership draws the line explicitly, communicates it with a business risk framing rather than a compliance framing, and gives employees approved alternatives so the productivity need doesn't push them back to consumer tools.

**When it fails:** You announce a policy without providing approved tooling. Employees comply in meetings and use consumer tools when they need to get something done.

## Why AI Mandates Create the Resistance You're Trying to Overcome

Metric-driven AI adoption mandates read differently to employees than they do to leadership. A mandate that tracks adoption rates tells employees that AI is a performance monitoring tool, not a capability upgrade. The resistance is rational. People protect their workflows from surveillance.

Improving's training and adoption model operates differently: give people a range of options and help them find where AI genuinely elevates work they already care about doing well. The framing shifts from "here's what you must use" to "here's what this makes possible for you specifically." That shift determines whether adoption becomes self-sustaining or requires perpetual enforcement.

**When this works:** Early adopters who find genuine value become internal evangelists. Approved tooling and use-case clarity precede the cultural push.

**When it fails:** Mandates arrive before tooling or training. Employees adopt the motion of using AI without getting the substance.

## Where You Actually Are Is Where You Start

The AI maturity picture Rusic describes has three rough levels. Level 1: using AI as a research assistant and thinking partner, where context quality determines output quality. Level 2: AI connected to internal data in a governed, access-controlled framework. Level 3: agents that execute tasks with reduced human instruction, the highest leverage and the highest risk, and the area where, as Rusic put it, nobody has fully figured it out yet.

Most organizations are at Level 1 for individuals and have not yet built the data foundation for Level 2. That is a valid place to be. The shift is real and it is not reversing. Anyone holding out for a return to the status quo is making a costly bet.

The businesses that come out of this period ahead asked what they were already doing well, figured out where AI compounds that, built the data foundation that makes it secure, and gave their people the tools and the policy clarity to use it. A roofer with an AI targeting tool is still a roofer. They are just the roofer who gets there first.
