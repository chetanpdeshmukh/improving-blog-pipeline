# Your Business DNA Is the Strategy. AI Is the Multiplier.

The roofing company in John Rusic's story has no data scientists. No machine learning engineers. No AI strategy deck with a three-year roadmap. What they have is a startup that figured out roofers need to knock on doors within 48 hours of a hailstorm, and that AI can tell them exactly which neighborhoods to target before the competition shows up.

"I've spent the last several years watching businesses try to add AI to things that were working fine and abandon initiatives that could have compounded what they were already doing well," says Rusic, Technical Director at Improving's AI Practice. The organizations that get results ask a different first question: what do we already do well, and where does AI help us do it faster or at a scale we couldn't reach before? The organizations that don't get results ask what AI they should deploy.

That framing difference is the one most organizations never name before they are already inside the wrong initiative. Rusic has watched it play out repeatedly: teams that asked what AI could amplify in something they were already doing well compounded results. Teams that started with "what AI should we deploy" spent budget without compounding anything.

## Where AI Earns Its Paycheck

Improving worked with an education company sitting on a massive library of content: videos, PowerPoint presentations, classroom aids built over years of curriculum development. Clients who paid for access to that content couldn't effectively find what was relevant to them. The library existed, but its value was locked behind the limits of keyword search.

The pressure was straightforward: clients needed to self-serve and locate material specific to their context at the moment they needed it. Manually curating content for each client wasn't economically viable. Improving used AI to index and semantically search across that unstructured content, making it discoverable in ways traditional keyword search couldn't touch. A client looking for curriculum material on a specific topic could surface relevant resources from a library that would have taken hours to manually browse. The roofing example makes the principle more visible. The decision of what to do with that information stays with your people. A human reviewer needs to sit at the end of that process for anything customer-facing — AI still fabricates, and skipping that review trades accuracy for speed, which is rarely the bargain you intended.

It fails when the content or data you're surfacing is ungoverned. AI returns whatever it can reach, to whoever asks the right question. Which leads directly to the next problem.

## The Foundation Nobody Wants to Build First

Every AI capability worth building sits on top of a data estate most companies have not built yet. That is a business strategy problem wearing IT clothes.

The starting question is blunt: do you know what data you have, where it lives, who can access it, and what is sensitive? Most organizations cannot answer all four parts with confidence. They discover the gaps when they connect AI to their data and something surfaces that should not have.

The specific failure mode: an AI tool without internal access controls returns anything it can reach to whoever asks the right question. A marketing team member queries the internal knowledge base and the response includes executive compensation data. The outer security perimeter held. The inner ring fences did not exist. This is a data governance gap that became visible the moment you gave employees a tool smart enough to find the information.

Third-party data compounds the risk. If your HR platform or SaaS tools are integrated into your data estate, their data governance terms are now your liability too.

You find out your data estate is ungoverned one of two ways: through an audit, or through an incident. Only one of those is recoverable with a reasonable remediation plan.

## The Consumer AI Line Your Organization Must Draw

There is a material difference between an employee using a personal ChatGPT account and an employee using an enterprise Azure OpenAI deployment. Consumer AI tools run on a data business model. Productivity is the hook, not the product. An employee who pastes a client report or internal financial summary into a consumer AI tool has potentially donated that data to a model training process. Enterprise clients report this happening far more often than organizations realize. Rusic's reaction when given one industry estimate: "That seems low to me."

Enterprise agreements purchase training data exclusion, processing boundaries, and contractual accountability. They don't guarantee perfect protection, but they represent a materially different risk profile than consumer terms.

The majority of organizations have no AI or GPT-specific policy. They are letting employees navigate this distinction individually, without guidance, at productivity speeds.

Leadership that communicates the policy with a business risk framing and provides approved alternatives immediately keeps employees from navigating the distinction on their own. A policy-without-tooling announcement achieves the opposite: compliance in meetings, consumer tools in practice.

## Why AI Mandates Create the Resistance You're Trying to Overcome

Metric-driven AI adoption mandates read differently to employees than they do to leadership. A mandate that tracks adoption rates tells employees that AI is a surveillance tool. The resistance is rational. People protect their workflows from surveillance.

Improving's training and adoption model works differently: give people a range of options and help them find where AI makes the specific work they already care about faster or more accurate. The framing shifts from "here's what you must use" to "here's what this makes possible for you specifically." That shift determines whether adoption becomes self-sustaining or requires perpetual enforcement. Mandates that arrive before tooling or training guarantee the resistance. When approved tooling and use-case clarity come first, early adopters who find genuine value become internal evangelists and adoption compounds without enforcement.

## Where You Actually Are Is Where You Start

The AI maturity picture Rusic describes has three rough levels. Level 1: using AI as a research assistant and thinking partner, where context quality determines output quality. Level 2: AI connected to internal data in a governed, access-controlled framework. Level 3: agents that execute tasks with reduced human instruction — the highest leverage and the highest risk, and the area where, as Rusic put it, nobody has fully figured it out yet.

Most organizations are at Level 1 for individuals and have not yet built the data foundation for Level 2. That is a valid place to be. The shift is real and it is accelerating. Anyone holding out for a return to the status quo is making a costly bet.

The businesses that come out of this period ahead asked what they were already doing well, figured out where AI compounds that, built the data foundation that makes it secure, and gave their people the tools and the policy clarity to use it. A roofer with an AI targeting tool is still a roofer. They are just the roofer who gets there first.
